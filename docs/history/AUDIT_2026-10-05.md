## 3. Findings

### F1. The 3% rule leaves feed errors behind, and v2's per-candidate results depend on them (verified)

**What I did.** I ran the same rule at lower thresholds. Wicks beyond their body and neighbours:

| Distance beyond the reference | 1–1.5% | 1.5–2% | 2–2.5% | 2.5–3% | > 3% (corrected) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cells | 146 | 87 | 51 | 25 | 62 |

The 3% threshold does not sit in a gap: the count rises smoothly below it. Many cells between 1.5% and 3% are clearly the same kind of error. Some come from the same bad print as a corrected neighbour: 2008-10-16 10:54–10:56 have the same $91.27 high as the corrected 10:58 bar but sit at 2.8–3.0%. Several are in or near the walk-forward period:

| Bar | Wick | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 2011-12-20 13:30 | low 120.37 | neighbours 123.70–123.75 (−2.7%) |
| 2014-12-18 15:59 | high 212.97 | neighbours 206.6–206.8 (+3.0%, just under the threshold). It enters `cum_max` / ATH features until the real price passes it on 2015-05-18, so it also affects fold 0's validation quarter |
| 2020-03-16 14:45, 2020-03-18 10:05 / 10:14 | lows 240.78 / 240.00, high 248.37 | single-bar spikes of 1.8–2.8% |
| 2020-05-14 09:40 | low 272.99 | neighbours ~278.4 (−1.9%) |
| 2025-05-27 13:13–13:14 | lows 578.43 and 585.38 | neighbours ~590 (−2.0%, and a sibling at −0.8%) |
| 2026-06-26 15:59 | low 716.58 | close 729.51, previous close 733.40 (−2.3%). This is in the last test window |

Not every bar is an error: the 2010-05-06 flash-crash bars are real and also appear at 1.5–2.5%. A lower threshold alone is therefore not a correct rule either.

**Why it matters.** Anything triggered by a minute **low** reads these wicks: v1's SL labels (distances of 0.1–1%: any open position is stopped by a 2% fake low) and v2's trailing stop. High-based features (`prev_day_range_pct`, `high60/250_dist_pct`, ATH distance) are affected too.

**Sensitivity of v2.** I applied the same rule at 2% and at 1.5% (in memory; v2 rules unchanged) and re-ran all 792 validation candidates:

| Cleaning | Cells cut | Best candidate after the fact | Candidates above buy-and-hold (+243.4%) | Prior-only selection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none (uncorrected) | 0 | +195.0% (all, k = 5, 0) | 0 of 18 | +141.1% (23 of 43) |
| 3% (official) | 62 | +229.7% (all, k = 5, 0) | 0 of 18 | +111.7% (20 of 43) |
| 2% | 138 | +227.7% (all, k = 4, 0) | 0 of 18 | +103.1% (19 of 43) |
| 1.5% | 225 | **+276.3%** (all, k = 4, 0) | **1 of 18** | +104.2% (19 of 43) |

Changing about 0.01% of the bars changed 189 of the 792 candidate-quarters, some by more than 10 points (e.g. fold 20, all/k = 4/0: −3.8% → +7.7%). This happens because a single stop event changes everything after it, and because the model's inputs change.

**What this supports.**
- The **v2 verdict is robust.** The honest prior-only number loses badly at every cleaning level.
- The **ranking of candidates, and any after-the-fact "best", is mostly noise.** Had the 1.5% version been the official data, the RESULTS_LOG would show one candidate above buy-and-hold. In plain terms: the gap between candidates is smaller than the noise from tiny data details, so "which setting won" carries almost no information.
- For v3:
  - decide the cleaning before any v3 result exists, for example a stricter rule that also flags repeated identical wick prices or checks against a second data source;
  - make a **data-perturbation check** part of the protocol: any claimed success must survive the alternative cleanings;
  - consider triggering stops on minute **closes** rather than lows, which makes them insensitive to single-bar wicks. That is a design change, so it needs a decision and a protocol entry.

### F2. What the 2015–2026 data can and cannot show (verified arithmetic, inference on implications)

From the 44 quarterly validation excess returns of each v2 candidate:
- the standard deviation of the quarterly excess return is 2.0–6.6 percentage points (median 4.0);
- the smallest **true** mean excess that a two-sided 5% test would detect 80% of the time (the minimum detectable effect) is 0.9–2.8 points per quarter, i.e. about **+3.4 to +11 points per year**, with a median of +6.7.

In plain terms: even if a strategy really beat SPY by 2% a year, which would be an excellent result, 11 years of quarterly data would usually not be enough to show it convincingly. The protocol's "statistically supported" label (lower bound of the 95% monthly-bootstrap interval above 0) will almost certainly never be reached by any realistic strategy on this data. **That is a property of the data, not a flaw of the code.** The thesis should say so before reporting v3, so that a "not statistically supported" result is read correctly.

The flip side: several v2 candidates were **reliably worse** than buy-and-hold (mean quarterly excess with t ≈ −2 to −3.2, e.g. 10y / k = 4 / 0: t = −3.2). The data can detect harm more easily than help, because the stop's cost is systematic.

Implications for the design of v3 (for discussion, not decided):
- the test that can be informative is the **information test** (model vs same-exit random or fixed-delay re-entry, with matched time in cash). It is noisy too, but it isolates what Nicolas wants to know ("does information help?");
- report effect sizes with intervals rather than pass/fail;
- more independent data helps far more than more modelling (see F6 and §6).

### F3. v1's shortfall fits zero-skill partial exposure, not "capping winners" (inference, partly checked)

The handover says capping winners at +δ gave up the large up-moves. A symmetric barrier exit does not by itself lower expected return: the expected gain of a trade is roughly the drift multiplied by the time held, whatever the barriers. What lowers return relative to buy-and-hold is **the time spent out of the market** (after each exit) and costs.

A rough check:
- If a strategy has no skill and is invested a fraction f of the time, its log growth is about f × buy-and-hold's.
- With v1's buy-and-hold at +230% (ln 3.30 = 1.19), f = 0.76–0.95 gives an expected **+148% to +211%**.
- v1's best setting, +158%, corresponds to f ≈ 0.79, at the low end of the reported "invested 76–95%".
- So v1 behaves like random exposure to SPY about 80% of the time: no skill, and no special harm from the barriers.

This is consistent with the signal check (0 bins above the base rate on both windows). It changes the lesson recorded in RESULTS_LOG / HANDOVER §10.2 ("capping upside loses"). The better lesson is: **being out of the market costs drift, and entries chosen without skill cannot recover it.** I could not verify the exposure of the best setting itself (the per-setting exposure is not in the saved outputs). Nicolas can check it from `fold*_selection_data.csv` (column `valid_exposure_pct`, time held in bars ÷ window bars) in a minute.

### F4. v2's design could not test the mechanism it was built for (observed in code, inference on consequences)

v2's rationale was: be out during declines and back in before the recovery. Two rules limit the time out of the market:
- **Forced re-entry after 60 cash decisions** (about 3 months);
- **every 3-month window starts invested** (`simulate_stop_reentry_dict` buys at the window's first 10:00 open), so the chained validation series is forced back in every quarter.

Large drawdowns that a timing rule could avoid usually last longer than one quarter: 2008 lasted about 17 months, 2022 about 9. In the 2015–2026 validation, the strategy could therefore never stay out through 2022. Its tests covered **short exits** only.

The negative result is still valid for what was tested ("stop, then re-enter within 60 sessions"). It should not be generalised to "risk-based exits cannot beat buy-and-hold". It also explains part of the "insurance pattern": small gains in down quarters, capped by forced re-entry. The MA200 rule in step 10 (no forced re-entry, one 10-year window) shows the other side: 73% of buy-and-hold's return with a much smaller drawdown, but it bought back lower in only 1 of 33 episodes (computed by me). That one episode (out 2007-12-27, back in 2009-06-01) gained +55.6% in share count. The other 32 together lost about 43% of the share count (sum of log share gains −0.56). Its whole benefit rests on one bear market.

If v3 keeps "exit, then re-enter", Nicolas should decide explicitly whether the forced re-entry and the window reset stay, and why. Each choice has costs: without them, results depend on one or two bear markets.

### F5. AUC below 0.5 for the "all history" model: a possible regime effect (inference)

I fitted the logistic model on all history to 2015-03-18 (training data only) and looked at which conditions lower its predicted P(up). Its probabilities correlate with trend (distance from the 200-session average +0.47, from the 60-session high +0.47, 60-session return +0.40) and against **volatility** (−0.51). The highest-volatility fifth of days gets a mean P of 0.57, against 0.63–0.69 for the other fifths. In plain terms, the model learned "below trend and volatile means stay out". That is what 2008 taught, when volatile declines kept falling. (The individual coefficients are hard to read because the features are strongly correlated, so I used the correlations of the fitted probabilities instead.) In 2015–2026, volatility spikes (2018, 2020, 2025) were mostly followed by fast rebounds, so this model ranks validation days slightly in the wrong direction (AUC 0.470, above 0.5 in 17 of 44 folds, p = 0.17).

On its own, the AUC shortfall is not significant. It does fit the step 10 post-exit evidence (stops sell near the lows of dips that revert). Together they suggest that, outside 2008, short-horizon **reversal after stress** dominated in this SPY sample. This is an interpretation, not a tested result. This matters for v3: VIX is mostly a better volatility measure, so a VIX model trained with 2008 inside its window may learn the same "high fear means stay out" rule that failed after 2015. Whether the sign is stable across regimes is the main empirical risk of v3. (Reversal after stress is also a known pattern in the literature, so this is not a new discovery to claim.)

### F6. How much data is genuinely unseen (agree with handover §9; additions)

- I agree that almost every quarter from 2015 to 2026-04 has been used as validation data and has influenced design decisions. The 2015–2026 period is **development data**, not out-of-sample data.
- **The fold schedule is anchored on the last session of the data** (`get_walk_forward_fold_pdf`). When new data arrives, every window shifts. "The test windows" are therefore not a fixed set of dates. The v3 protocol should name a **calendar holdout start date** instead (e.g. "everything after 2026-08-13 is holdout").
- The last test window (2026-05-14 → 2026-08-13, about 63 sessions) is far too short to test a quarterly strategy against buy-and-hold (F2). It is better kept as a sanity check than as the final verdict.
- Practical sources of fresh data, in my view the most valuable change before v3:
  1. **Other liquid US ETFs** (QQQ, IWM, DIA; possibly EFA/EEM) from IBKR. Nothing in the project has looked at them. A rule frozen on SPY and evaluated once on them is a real out-of-sample test of the idea. They are correlated with SPY, so not independent, but untouched.
  2. **SPY daily data 1993–2004** (includes the 2000–2002 bear market). This works for daily-decision rules; the intraday stop would need an approximation from daily lows.
  3. **Forward data** (paper trading from a frozen design): clean but slow.
- The protocol could also report a multiple-testing-aware statistic over the trial log (e.g. a deflated Sharpe ratio or a reality-check style comparison across all logged candidates). This is optional.

### F8. Dividends, cash interest and what "+243%" means (inference; figures approximate)

- All buy-and-hold figures are **price returns**. With SPY's dividends (yield roughly 1.2–2% a year over 2015–2026; please verify with the actual distributions), buy-and-hold's total return over the validation span would be roughly +300% rather than +243%. The thesis should label the numbers "price return, dividends excluded".
- For relative results the effect is small on average. Dividends missed while in cash (about 1.6% a year) and the interest that cash would earn (near 0% in 2015–2016 and 2020–2021, 4–5% in 2023–2025) roughly offset over the decade. Year by year they do not offset, and the effect grows with time in cash. If v3 spends more time in cash, both should be modelled (dividend dates and a T-bill rate), which is cheap to add.
- Unadjusted prices also put a ~0.3% overnight gap on every ex-dividend date. That gap triggers small v1 stop-losses and shows up in v2's returns as a "loss" that a real holder would have received back as cash.

## 4. Alignment: TP rate vs beating buy-and-hold (handover §1.3)

My view, close to the previous assistant's:

- **"TP is reached substantially more often than SL" is not a meaningful aim on its own.** It can be met without skill: a TP closer than the SL, or symmetric barriers during SPY's drift (v1: being long already beat the break-even TP rate at 0.25–0.9%). The meaningful version is "the TP rate **after a signal** is above the **unconditional** TP rate at the same distance, after costs". v1 tested exactly that and found nothing (19 of 5,691 bins above the base rate on training, 0 on both windows). **The original aim, stated properly, has also failed.** The change of criterion is therefore not why the results are negative, and the thesis can say so plainly.
- **"Total return above buy-and-hold" is the right economic question but a very demanding statistical one** (F2). It also mixes two things: whether the information helps, and whether the exit rule helps. The same-exit random and fixed-delay baselines are what separate them, and they should stay mandatory.
- **The project description should be updated.** Suggested wording (Nicolas decides):

> *Develop and evaluate, with a pre-registered protocol, a long-only SPY strategy that enters on information and exits on a calculated risk tolerance. Primary criterion: total return after costs above buy-and-hold over the same out-of-sample period. Secondary: higher Sharpe ratio and smaller maximum drawdown at ≥ 90% of buy-and-hold's return. The value of the information is judged against the same exit rule with uninformed (random / fixed-delay) re-entry. Negative results are reported as results. (Originally: SL/TP levels such that TP is hit more often than SL; replaced on 2026-10-04 because a high TP rate can be obtained without skill.)*

## 5. Is "beat buy-and-hold on SPY, long only, no leverage" achievable?

Honest opinion: **beating buy-and-hold on total return, with price-derived or volatility information, and showing it with statistical support on SPY alone, is very unlikely.** Being out of the market costs drift (F3). Short exits sell into reversals (v2, F5). The data cannot confirm small edges (F2). What is realistically achievable and well documented is **risk reduction at some cost in return** (MA200 in step 10: 73% of the return, less than half the drawdown, mostly from one episode).

Nicolas has decided that the primary criterion stays, and that is a legitimate choice for a thesis: a carefully documented negative answer is a valid result. My suggestions:
- (a) keep the primary criterion, but state its power limits up front (F2);
- (b) give the **information test** (model vs same-exit random re-entry, matched time in cash) equal prominence;
- (c) do not loosen any criterion after seeing v3 results.

## 6. Opinion on v3 (VIX), and what to settle before it starts

**Worth doing?** Yes, as a clearly scoped test: "does implied-volatility information improve re-entry timing over the same exit with uninformed re-entry?" VIX is the first input that is not derived from SPY's own prices, so it is a genuinely new kind of information. Expectations should be modest (F5).

**Must be in the v3 protocol before any data is explored:**
1. **Timing.**
   - VIX and VIX3M are calculated until 16:15 ET, so a daily close is not known at the 15:58 decision.
   - Use the previous session's close, or intraday index bars at 15:58 if IBKR provides them for both indices (to be checked).
   - Add a test that shifting VIX one day earlier changes nothing (the same truncation test as for the daily features).
2. **History.**
   - VIX3M (formerly VXV) daily history starts on **2007-12-04**. Any term-structure feature is missing for 2005–2007.
   - Decide how the exploration window (2005–2014) and the first folds' training windows handle this: start later, or use VIX only.
   - Starting in December 2007 also means the training data begins with the 2008 crash, which makes the regime problem of F5 worse.
3. **Data cleaning** fixed in advance, and a **data-perturbation check** as part of the success criteria (F1).
4. **Holdout**: a calendar date, plus at least one untouched instrument (F6).
5. **Exit and re-entry constraints** (forced re-entry, window reset): confirm or change them explicitly (F4).
6. **Target alignment.** The re-entry decision asks "will buying later be cheaper than buying now?". The v2 label asks "is the 20-session return positive?". The protocol already compares the probability with the base rate. A target closer to the decision (e.g. the forward return relative to drift, or whether the price in the next n sessions trades below today's fill) is worth a deliberate choice. This is a design change, so it needs an explicit decision.
7. **Sell at strength** (open decision): my view is to leave it out of v3. It has 30 exploration trials, about 15 episodes and a sign flip between thresholds. If Nicolas wants it, it belongs in its own pre-registered protocol evaluated on the fresh-data holdout, not folded into v3.
8. The `min_month_count_in` guard, the trial-log naming, and `config_v3.py` as already agreed.
